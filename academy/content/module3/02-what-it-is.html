Key message: OGA shows independent characterisation data. Whether an antibody works in your experiment is still yours to show.

Every result on OGA comes from YCharOS, an academic–industry consortium. For each protein, YCharOS obtains the commercially available antibodies and tests them side by side in the same cells: a wild-type line that makes the protein, and a knockout line that does not (for a few genes, a knockdown instead). The experiments follow community consensus protocols published in Nature Protocols (2024), and each gene's full results are published as a report on F1000Research or Zenodo.

Five applications are covered:

  • WB — western blot
  • IP — immunoprecipitation
  • ICC-IF — immunofluorescence on cultured cells
  • FC — flow cytometry
  • IHC — immunohistochemistry on wild-type and knockout cell pellets; for some genes also on mouse tissue, including knockout tissue

Not every gene has been tested in all five, and the gene page says which ones it has.

Two things set this data apart from a supplier's datasheet. The testing is independent of the manufacturer. And antibodies are tested in applications the datasheet may not claim, so a result can be supportive where the supplier says nothing, or not supportive where the supplier recommends it.

What OGA is not: a stamp saying an antibody is "validated". OGA characterises antibodies. A supportive result is evidence that supports the antibody's specificity in the conditions tested: those cells, that protocol. Your cells, your tissue, your fixation and your protocol are different, and showing the antibody works there is validation, which is your own work. Modules 1, 2 and 4 and the Validation Planning Framework are about doing it.

Everything on OGA is free, and the figures are released under a CC BY 4.0 licence: reuse them, and cite the report they came from.
