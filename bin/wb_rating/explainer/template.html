How the blot measurement works

The prototype turns a published western blot crop into a handful of numbers, then applies your rules to those numbers. This page shows each step on your own blots, defines every number, and shows which numbers line up with the decisions you have made. Everything here was computed from the 195 of your 212 decided blots the program could read.

  1. Find the blot

    The program looks for rectangles drawn as dark frame lines: pixels darker than 120 on a 0–255 grey scale, in straight runs. A frame needs two matching vertical sides and a top or bottom edge between them. A pink panel (Ponceau) is skipped, because its red channel is well above its green. Of what is left, it takes the largest frame starting in the top 60% of the crop. Nothing outside that frame is read, so the ladder and the labels play no part.

  2. Split it into lanes

    The frame is cut into equal-width lanes. It tries 2, 3, 4 and 6 lanes and keeps the count where lane centres are darkest compared with the gaps between them. Only the middle half of each lane is read, which keeps smudges at the lane edges out. Only two-lane blots are rated, and the left lane is taken to be WT. With more lanes the program would need to be told which lane is which.

  3. Read darkness down each lane

    For every pixel row, darkness is 255 − brightness: 0 is white, 255 is black. The row's value is the median across the strip, so one speck does not count. The result is the left chart: one line per lane, top of the blot at the top.

  4. Take away the background floor

    Smear and uneven background raise the whole line. The floor (dashed) is the lowest value within a window of ±k rows, smoothed, where k is 8% of the blot height (at least 15 rows). It follows slow changes but cannot rise into a band narrower than the window. Subtracting it gives the right chart. A broad smear is partly removed with the floor, which is why smear has its own number below.

  5. Find the bands and compare the lanes

    A band is a peak in the WT line at least units high. For each one the program records its height, its prominence (how far it stands above the dip that separates it from any taller neighbour), and the KO value: the highest point of the KO line within 3 rows of the band. Ratio = KO ÷ WT. A ratio of 0.05 means 5% of the WT band is left in the KO.

  6. Apply the rules

    The ratios and heights go through your rules in order, each judgement replaced by a threshold. The numbers for this blot, and the path it took, are below.

    What this shows

    The drowned test the rules use now hardly varies. It is the target band's prominence as a share of its own height. After the floor is removed, almost every band stands fully clear of its neighbours, so the value sits near 1.00 whichever way you rated the blot. A band can be weak beside much stronger bands and still score 1.00, which is the ACE GTX100923 case.

    Shares of signal do better. The target band's share of all the signal in the WT lane separates supportive from Not supportive best of any single number here. The share of the lane that disappears in the KO comes next. Both put a number on what you described: the target is weak relative to everything else in the lane.

    No one number is enough on its own. Adding any of them to the current rules as an extra "drowned" cut moves agreement by one blot at most (139 → 140 exact). The groups overlap: plenty of blots you rated "Supportive, but not selective" have a small target share too. The chart below shows that overlap with two numbers at once.

      Truncations, and what they mean for IF

      Your rule: a band that drops to a smaller size in the KO is lost, because the knockout makes a truncated protein. The prototype looks for this as a band that is reduced in the KO with a band below it that appears only in the KO, within 16% of the blot height.

      It flags of the 195 blots, and you rated every one "Supportive, but not selective". The test is crude, so these are candidates to check by eye, not findings: .

      Your note, 3 Oct: where a knockout makes a truncated protein, the antibody may still find it in the knockout cells. Immunofluorescence on those cells would then look non-specific because of the knockout line, not the antibody. So for an antibody whose western blot shows a truncation, a Not supportive IF result should not be shown.

      Nothing on the site records a truncation today; it lives only in the blot image. Building this needs a stored mark per antibody, set by a person (the measurement can suggest it), and the gene page, the API, the extension and the MCP then hiding or annotating that antibody's IF result. It is recorded in the roadmap, Workstream H.

      What the numbers cannot see

      • Which lane is which. WT is assumed to be the left lane, and lanes are assumed to be equal widths across the frame.
      • Molecular weight. The ladder is outside the frame and is not read, so "directly beside" is measured in pixels: within 8% of the blot height.
      • Exposure and contrast. Every figure was exposed and saved differently, so a height of 200 on one blot is not a height of 200 on another. Ratios within one blot compare fairly; raw heights across blots do not. Saturated (pure black) bands flatten at the top, which understates the strongest bands.
      • Height, not amount. A band is read at its peak, not by the area under it. A fat band and a thin band of the same peak darkness count the same.
      • Knockdown. Whether a blot is a knockdown comes from a person's flag, not from the lane labels.

      Decisions for you

      1. Should the drowned test (rule 2) use the target's share of the lane's signal instead of its prominence? If so, the cut-off has to come from blots you rate fresh, not these 195, or it is fitted to the answers.
      2. Should "faint" (rule 5) stay relative to the target band (now: below of its height), or become relative to the whole lane?
      3. Is the shift test worth tightening into a suggestion people confirm, so the IF rule has something to read?
