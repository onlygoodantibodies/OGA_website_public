Independent characterisation data for the antibodies a paper relies on

Most antibodies in the literature have never been tested against a knockout. This marks the ones that have: every mention on the page, coloured for how the antibody performed in the application that paper used it for. Hover any one of them to see the data behind it. Performance is assay- and sample-dependent, so this is context, not a verdict on the paper — worth most where a manuscript shows no context-specific validation of its own.

What it does

Open a paper on a journal site, a preprint server or Europe PMC and every antibody in the methods that YCharOS has characterised is underlined, right there in the text. Hover it to see how it did in each application — western blot, immunoprecipitation, immunofluorescence, flow cytometry — alongside the wild-type-versus-knockout image, the clone, the RRID and a link to the report.

Antibodies reported to recognise the wrong target are flagged too (what that means).

Switch on Also run on supplier catalogue pages and you can see while you shop whether an independently characterised antibody exists for your target. A toolbar panel adds up the whole page and lists alternatives. Any card can hand the paper to your own AI assistant through the OGA connector, to work out which application was used and whether the authors ran controls of their own.

Why “run on any site” is worth granting

Without it, the extension only works on a fixed list of publisher addresses — and a great deal of real reading never matches that list. Papers opened through your institution's proxy arrive at a rewritten address (nature.com becomes something like nature-com.ezproxy…), which no list can anticipate. Society journals and the long tail of smaller publishers are not on it either.

None of those pages get marked, and — this is the part worth knowing — nothing on screen tells you so. It looks exactly like a paper with no characterised antibodies in it. When we measured this against a corpus of real papers, eleven of the 34 publisher addresses were not on the default list.

Granting it costs you no privacy. The extension does not read pages and send them anywhere — it holds the dataset and the matching rules inside your own browser, so a broader permission means it can look at more of what is already in front of you, and nothing more. Nothing about what you read leaves the machine either way.

The antibody that is for a different protein

Some antibodies are sold against one protein and routinely bought for another that shares its name. ab51243 is an antibody to ARPC5, also called p16-ARC, a subunit of the actin-related protein 2/3 complex — and Abcam's own datasheet states there is no observed cross-reactivity with p16-INK4a, the CDKN2A tumour suppressor that “p16” almost always means in a senescence paper. A published review found 317 of 406 papers citing that code had used it as a p16-INK4a antibody, in Nature, Nature Medicine, Cancer Cell and eLife among others. The same has happened to eight antibodies against the E. coli LacZ β-galactosidase, used as markers of senescence-associated β-galactosidase, which is the mammalian GLB1 enzyme.

{% comment %} The same collision can run both ways — a PERK antibody used for p-ERK and a p-ERK antibody used for PERK — and that is two notices out of one article, because a notice holds one declared target. So the reader-facing unit is the REVIEW and `public_totals` counts distinct sources; counting notices would say four and mean nothing to anybody. {% endcomment %}

Whether an unlisted paper is marked at all depends on the list. Where a product is mostly misused — 317 of the 406 for ab51243 — naming the code is enough, and the card says the paper may have used it wrongly, with the tally behind it. Where it is not, the list marks only the papers the review actually names: ab65142 is a perfectly good PERK antibody and nearly every paper citing it used it correctly, so a caution on those would be a false accusation. The two PERK lists are set that way and the two For Better Science lists are not.

It is not a verdict on the antibody. OGA has characterised none of these {{ confusion_totals.codes }} products, and the card says so outright: no application results, no figure, nothing about how well it works. What is documented is which protein it is raised against.

A paper the review read and found correct gets no mark at all. Seventeen of the 406 used ab51243 properly, for ARPC5, and a red mark on one of those would be an accusation against a paper somebody had already checked. Seventy-two could not be reached behind a paywall, and those say the use could not be established rather than borrowing the majority's answer.

Where the data comes from

YCharOS, an open-science consortium that tests antibodies against knockout cell lines to a community protocol and publishes every result — negatives included — as a peer-reviewed report with a DOI. No supplier claims, no citation counts.

Wrong-target flags are separate, and are the one thing on the page that is not a characterisation result: they come from investigative reporting at For Better Science, and each one links to the original post.

What the results mean. Everything is tested under the same standard conditions, which is what makes it characterisation data rather than a verdict — antibodies behave differently in different assays and samples. It matters most when a paper shows no validation of its own. A paper with its own knockout or knockdown may be perfectly fine using an antibody that struggled in our hands.

It does not watch you read

The dataset is downloaded and stored in your browser, and all matching happens there. No page text, URL or reading history is sent to us or to anyone else. An unpublished or under-review manuscript stays private.

The extension contacts this site for exactly two things: a once-a-day refresh of the characterisation data, and loading a characterisation image when you open a card. Neither carries any information about what you are reading.

No account, no analytics, and no server that knows what you read.

If your institution blocks extensions

Many universities and institutes manage Chrome, Edge and Firefox centrally and allow only approved extensions. If Add to Chrome does nothing, or Firefox refuses the install, that is usually what has happened — and it is a decision your IT team can reverse. They generally need three things: what it does, what it can reach, and its identifier.

A note you can send them

I'd like to install a browser extension called Only Good Antibodies — characterisation. It marks antibodies cited in a paper with what independent, knockout-controlled testing found, which helps me avoid buying reagents that have been shown not to work.

It stores its dataset locally and does all matching in the browser. No page text, URL or reading history is sent anywhere — the manifest declares no data collection. It contacts one domain, onlygoodantibodies.co.uk, about twice a day to refresh that dataset and to load an image when I open a card.

It asks for three permissions — storage, alarms and scripting — and by default runs only on a published list of {{ site_count }} journal and preprint domains. It has no access to tabs, cookies, browsing history or downloads. Anything wider is optional and off unless I turn it on.

The details you'll need to allow it are at https://onlygoodantibodies.co.uk/extension/.

For an administrator who already manages browsers. Allowing lets people install it if they want to; force-installing deploys it to everyone. Either is fine — nothing about the extension needs it to be mandatory.

"ExtensionSettings": {
  "{{ gecko_id }}": {

Use force_installed in place of allowed to deploy it to everyone. Firefox needs install_url either way, because the add-on is not listed on addons.mozilla.org.

What it can and cannot tell you

  • It reports what independent testing found. It does not judge the paper.
  • Most antibodies have never been independently tested. Grey means we have no result for the application used here.
  • Which application a reagent was used for is inferred from the surrounding text. Where a page is ambiguous the extension falls back to a whole-antibody summary and says so on the card.
  • A paper that shows its own knockout or knockdown may be perfectly fine using an antibody that struggled in our hands. The mark is about the reagent under one protocol, not about the experiment in front of you.
  • Every card links to the knockout-controlled images, the RRID and the published report, so you can check the colour yourself.
